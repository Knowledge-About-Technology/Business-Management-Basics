How to Make a Cash Flow Forecast for a Small Business

Cash flow forecasting helps you estimate whether the business will have enough cash when bills are due. The key is to forecast timing—not just whether a sale or expense appears profitable.

What a cash flow forecast shows

A cash flow forecast estimates the cash available at the beginning of a period, expected receipts, expected payments, and the balance at the end. It is a forward-looking planning tool; it does not guarantee that every payment will arrive on time.

Forecasting is especially useful for businesses with seasonal sales, long customer payment terms, inventory purchases, project-based revenue, or uneven expenses.

Choose a forecast horizon

A short-term weekly forecast can help manage immediate liquidity, while a monthly forecast can support broader planning. The right horizon depends on how quickly the business's cash position changes and how far ahead it can reasonably predict receipts and payments.

Use a rolling forecast: when one week or month ends, add another period at the far end. Keep the forecast simple enough to update consistently.

Step 1: Record opening cash

Start with cash actually available for business use, reconciled to bank records. Do not automatically count unused credit limits, customer promises, or restricted funds as cash. If several bank accounts are involved, document which balances are included and avoid double-counting transfers between them.

Step 2: Estimate cash coming in

List expected customer payments by likely receipt date, not merely invoice date. Include point-of-sale receipts, subscription collections, grants, financing proceeds, or other inflows only when they are relevant and reasonably supported.

For invoices, consider contractual terms, customer payment history, disputes, and collection risk. A conservative forecast may show expected and delayed-payment scenarios separately. Do not count the same sale twice when an invoice is issued and then paid.

Step 3: Estimate cash going out

List payroll, rent, supplier invoices, taxes, loan principal and interest, insurance, software renewals, inventory, equipment, owner withdrawals, and other planned payments. Use due dates and realistic payment timing. Include irregular costs rather than spreading them invisibly across months if the actual cash leaves in one payment.

Distinguish cash expenses from non-cash accounting expenses. For example, depreciation generally does not represent a payment in the period, while purchasing equipment does create a cash outflow.

Step 4: Calculate the closing balance

For each period, calculate: opening cash + expected cash inflows − expected cash outflows = closing cash. The closing balance becomes the next period's opening balance. Check formulas and ensure internal transfers are not counted as both an inflow and an outflow in a way that changes total cash.

Compare projected cash with the minimum balance needed to meet obligations. If a shortfall appears, record its date, size, cause, and the options available to address it.

Step 5: Test scenarios and act early

Create a cautious scenario where a large customer pays late, sales are lower, or a planned expense rises. Test a more favorable scenario too, but avoid using it as the only plan. A forecast is most valuable when it creates time to act.

Possible responses include accelerating legitimate invoice follow-up, negotiating supplier timing, reducing discretionary spending, delaying a nonessential purchase, arranging appropriate financing, or revising delivery plans. Check contractual, legal, and relationship consequences before changing payment dates.

Common cash-flow forecasting mistakes

Common errors include treating signed deals as cash already received, forgetting tax and annual renewal dates, using profit instead of cash timing, ignoring overdue invoices, and failing to reconcile the opening balance. Forecasts also become unreliable when nobody is responsible for updates.

Use actual bank activity to compare forecast with reality and improve the assumptions over time.

Weekly or monthly cash review

  • Reconcile the opening cash balance.
  • Review expected receipts and overdue invoices.
  • Confirm upcoming payroll, tax, debt, and supplier payments.
  • Identify any period below the minimum operating cash target.
  • Record mitigation actions and their owners.
  • Compare last forecast with actual cash and adjust assumptions.

Frequently Asked Questions

How far ahead should I forecast cash flow?

Use the horizon that matches your business's decision needs. Many businesses combine a detailed short-term forecast with a longer monthly outlook.

Is cash flow the same as profit?

No. Profit is measured under accounting rules, while cash flow tracks money received and paid. Credit sales, unpaid bills, equipment purchases, and loan principal can make the two differ.

What should I do if the forecast shows a cash shortage?

Identify the date and size of the gap, verify the assumptions, and evaluate options early. Do not assume financing will be available until terms and approval are confirmed.

Can a spreadsheet forecast cash flow?

Yes. A spreadsheet can work well when dates, formulas, assumptions, and responsibility for updates are clearly documented.
